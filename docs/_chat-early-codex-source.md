## Statement
**Eyebrow:** Statement
**Headline:** Gesture, captured. Gesture, cast.

Holo-Flow Studio began as twelve years of poi — the spun weight, the practised arc, fire held against the dark. The camera came later, to keep the gestures that would otherwise disappear at dawn. Then the gestures got programmed, precise, rendered in LED arrays built to draw whatever the hand could lift.

The **photographs** are the record: single long exposures of image data written into physical space by moving light, captured whole. No compositing.

The **objects** are what happens when those photographs go back into the physical. 3D-printed bodies whose geometry is derived from the captured trace, shot through with clear acrylic waveguides that carry internal light through the print. The gesture glows again from inside the body it drew.

Each piece is editioned, signed, and ships with the field record — where the gesture was performed, what hour, what date.

## Pipeline
**Eyebrow:** The pipeline
**Headline:** From a gesture in a field to an object on your shelf.

1. **Perform** — A kata held in a place that matters. Fire poi, programmed LED arrays, drone-mounted lights. One exposure.
2. **Capture** — The photograph holds what the eye and the room saw. Sometimes it's the final object. Sometimes it's the seed.
3. **Translate** — The photograph becomes a mesh. Voxel tracing, extrusion, boolean passes. The gesture becomes geometry.
4. **Print** — SLA-printed in resin. Clear acrylic waveguides grown through the body. LEDs seated at the waveguide roots.
5. **Finish** — Sanded, tinted, polished. Seated on its base. Powered, addressed, numbered. Packed with its field record.

# /about

## I — The practice — Twelve years of poi.

Poi is a movement discipline. Two weights on two cords, drawn through planes by the body. It takes years to make fluent — years before the weights stop ruling you and you start ruling them. I came to it for the dance, not the photograph.

The first few thousand hours were spent on the geometry itself. Cross-follow, antispin, weaves, arm combinations. Fire poi at the end of the first year. After that, the body knew what a planar gesture was. It just couldn't *keep* one.

Holo-Flow Studio is what happens when those trails are pulled out of the photograph and cast into something you can put on a shelf.

## II — The methods — Three ways to paint light, one way to hold it.

The photographs come from three different light-painting practices, each contributing different qualities to the archive.

**Traditional light painting.** Torches, LED wands, fire poi, handheld sticks. Organic, gestural, human-scale. Good for landscape pieces and for work where the body's presence in the gesture is part of the subject.

**Persistence-of-vision LED arrays.** Addressable LEDs on a moving chassis — programmed frame by frame, synced against a rotation sensor, able to render arbitrary image data *as light, in physical space.* The rigs are built in-studio around a Teensy microcontroller and TLC5927 drivers; the frames are designed ahead in software; the body walks the spatial trajectory. What the camera captures is not the body — it is the image data in its briefly-real physical form.

**Drone-mounted LED systems.** The studio's drone fleet (Mavic 2 Pro, Neo, Neo 2, Avata 360) is in service for aerial photography today, flown FPV through DJI Goggles, an RC 2 controller, an InAir head-tracking pod, and Xreal One Pro AR glasses. The airframes have now been modified with programmable LED arrays for aerial light painting — rings at altitude, traces over landscape, arcs across architecture, geometries too large for any body. The platform is flying. First test captures recording this season.

Link: "The full history of how these stacked up →" → /practice

## III — The objects — Photograph becomes geometry. Geometry becomes object.

The photographs are finished work in their own right, editioned and printed. But they are also source material. Each capture can be translated into 3D geometry — voxel-traced, extruded, refined as a mesh — and then SLA-printed in resin as an object.

Embedded in the body of every object: clear acrylic **ambient-light waveguides**, grown along the trace lines of the original gesture. A small warm LED seated at the waveguide root carries light through the print, edge-lighting the geometry from inside. The gesture that was drawn in the dark glows again, from inside the body it drew.

Three product lines: **desktop waveguides** (palm-scale, lit), **sculptures** (object-scale, lit or unlit), and **wall arrays** (configurable, commission).

## IV — The studio — Manchester, UK.

Holo-Flow Studio is based in Manchester. Dimona Dougherty — founder, technologist, twelve years into poi — runs the practice. The studio is a small one: one pair of hands, one workshop, small editions.

Everything is made to order, in runs. Nothing is stockpiled. Editions close on sell-through and never reopen. Each object carries its provenance: a card inside the shipping box naming the kata, the location, the hour, the date. If a track of music mattered to the gesture, it's named too.

# /practice

**Eyebrow:** A history of the practice
**Headline:** How light learned to hold **a shape.**
**Sub:** The tools changed. The question didn't. Each stage of the practice is a different answer to the same one: can a gesture last longer than the body that drew it?

## I — c. 2013 — Poi.
Poi is a movement discipline: two weights on two cords, drawn through planes by the body. It takes a long time to make fluent — years before the weights stop ruling you and you start ruling them. I came to it for the dance, not the photograph.

The first few thousand hours were spent on the geometry itself. Cross-follow, antispin, weaves, arm combinations. Fire poi at the end of the first year. After that, the body knew what a planar gesture was. It just couldn't *keep* one.

## II — c. 2015 — Long exposure.
Someone pointed a camera. A fifteen-second exposure of a fire kata — one single still of a minute's worth of spinning — and the thing the body had known became visible to everyone else. That was the hinge. Dance left a trace; the trace was worth keeping.

For the next few years, **traditional light painting** was the main tool: fire, LED wands, handheld torches, staff. Organic, gestural. A record of the body in motion. Still the practice most of the landscape photographs are made with.

## III — c. 2019 — Pixel poi.
The commercial LED pois began arriving — pixel poi, addressable strips on a chassis that would read an image file and replay it, column by column, as the poi swung. Suddenly a kata could carry a *picture*. You could swing a parrot; you could swing a word. Most of the scene used them as a party trick. A few of us started treating them as instruments.

What I wanted was to get the image quality up and the drift down. Commercial pois are designed for performance durability, not for photographic sharpness. So I started building.

## IV — c. 2021 — POV LED arrays.
A **persistence-of-vision LED array** is a display that doesn't look like a display until it moves. A strip of addressable LEDs, a microcontroller, a rotation or translation mechanism, a synchronisation signal. When it moves, it draws — one vertical slice of image per angular sample, a thousand times a second. The eye integrates. The camera integrates too.

My own rigs are built around a Teensy microcontroller, a bank of TLC5927 LED drivers, and Hall-effect sensors for rotational sync. One-hundred updates per revolution, at revolution rates the arm can sustain, means the image lands with photographic precision. I program the frames ahead of time; the body walks a trajectory; the long exposure captures the 3D volume the rig swept through.

That's the Holo-Flow signature: a photograph that is not of anything that ever stood still.

## V — 2025 → — Drones, modified.
The next move is up. The drone fleet is already in service for aerial photography — a Mavic 2 Pro for the photography-grade glass, a pair of Neos for follow-cam, an Avata 360 for FPV cinewhoop work, all flown through goggles and an RC 2 controller, head-tracked through an InAir pod with the live feed mirrored to Xreal One Pro AR glasses. Real flights, not hover-and-shoot.

The current build mounts **programmable LED arrays onto the airframes**: the rig freed from the body, able to draw at altitude, across landscape, around architecture. Think a ring of light held thirty metres above a moor at 04:30, tracing a kata too large for any performer.

The platform is operational. The LED-modified airframes are flying. *First test captures this evening.*

## VI — 2025 → — The objects.
The photographs became the source for a new line: 3D-printed objects whose geometry is *derived* from the captured light. A gesture that was drawn in air becomes a sculpture cast in resin.

Embedded in the body of every object: clear acrylic **ambient-light waveguides**, grown along the trace lines of the original gesture. A small LED seated at the waveguide root carries warm light through the print, edge-lighting the geometry from inside. The gesture that the body drew in the dark glows again from inside the object it made.

Desktop pieces, wall arrays, and one-off sculptures — every one an object of the photograph it was grown from.

## Summary
Holo-Flow Studio is the apparatus for holding gestures. Poi trained the body. Long exposure trained the camera. Pixel-level programming trained the eye to what light can carry. The objects are what those trainings make possible together: a sculpture, in a room, lit from inside by the residue of a gesture performed on a specific date, in a specific place, at a specific hour, held long enough to leave a mark.

# /shop/certificate

**Eyebrow:** Policy
**Headline:** Certificate of authenticity

Every print released ships with a single certificate on 300 gsm cotton rag, embossed with the studio seal and hand-signed.

The certificate records six facts: the work code, the edition reference, the edition number, the coordinates of the performance, the hour and date of the kata, and the studio's commitment that no further impressions of this edition will ever be made.

The certificate is the authoritative record. Works without their certificate are outside the archive. The archive maintains a private ledger of certificate numbers against owners, held only for the purpose of confirming provenance on resale.

## On reprints
No edition is re-opened. No work is re-issued in a different size, on a different paper, or under a different code. Two artist proofs are retained per edition and are not for sale.

# /shop/shipping

**Eyebrow:** Policy
**Headline:** Shipping & returns

## Dispatch
Works ship within 3–5 working days of order, rolled in an acid-free archival tube or, for objects, crated to size. All shipments are fully insured to declared value.

## Transit
UK: 2–3 working days, tracked. EU: 5–7 working days, tracked and signed. US & rest of world: 10–14 working days by courier. Import duties and local taxes are the recipient's responsibility.

## Returns
Because editions are small and every impression is accounted for, works are returnable only in the event of transit damage. Damaged items must be reported within 72 hours of delivery, with photographs of the tube or crate and the item itself. A replacement from the same edition will be sent wherever possible; where the edition has closed, a full refund will be issued.

## Collection
Works may be collected in person from the studio in Manchester by prior arrangement. Studio visits are by appointment only.
