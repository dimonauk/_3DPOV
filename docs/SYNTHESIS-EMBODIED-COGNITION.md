# Synthesis — Phenomenology of Presence and Embodied Cognition

*The cross-disciplinary lens that holds the studio's VR, flow-arts, and
Aura verticals together. Princess teaching register; quotes lifted
straight from the source where the source is sharper than any gloss.
For lifting into `/articles` or as the long-form companion to the
`phenomenology-of-presence` codex entry.*

---

## Why this doc exists

The studio's three public verticals — virtual reality work, flow-arts
practice, and the Aura companion — look, on the surface, like three
different products with three different audiences. They are not. They
are three positions of one phenomenon, and the phenomenon has a name
in two adjacent academic traditions that have been politely circling
each other for ninety years.

The two traditions are **phenomenology** (continental philosophy, from
Husserl forward) and **embodied cognition** (cognitive science, from
the 1980s forward). They were arguing about the same thing the whole
time. The studio uses the union of the two as its working theory of
why poi practice, VR composition, and the design of a digital
companion are the same craft, viewed from three positions of the
Loop.

This is the doc that says, plainly, what that theory is. It is also
the doc that says what it isn't — what the dark patterns look like,
where the accessibility gap is, what the studio refuses to do with
the leverage that presence affords.

It is roughly 3,500 words. The codex entry is the short version. The
articles in `/articles/vr-as-psychological-technology` and
`/articles/the-familiar` are the workshop-register versions. This is
the catalogue version: princess teaching mode, occasional quote, no
flourishes.

## 1. Phenomenology — the philosophical layer

### Husserl: bracketing, and "to the things themselves"

Edmund Husserl, lecturing in Göttingen and later Freiburg from
about 1900 onward, set himself the task of describing consciousness
without first asking the sciences for permission. His method, which
he called the *epoché* or **bracketing**, is the discipline of
setting aside, for the duration of the description, what one has been
told the experience under examination is. The famous slogan — *zu
den Sachen selbst*, "to the things themselves" — is an instruction
to stop summarising and start describing.

Bracketing is not scepticism. The bracketed beliefs are not denied;
they are placed in suspension while the experience is given a
chance to say what it is. This matters to the studio because the
two registers it most cares about — what a long-exposure photograph
*feels like* to look at, and what a VR scene *feels like* to inhabit
— are both impossible to describe honestly while the physics of the
camera or the frame-rate of the headset is in the foreground. Both
descriptions are useful. Both are different descriptions. Bracketing
is what keeps them from prematurely consuming one another.

The phenomenological method in its purest form is unwieldy and the
studio does not pretend to practise it formally. What it does
practise is the discipline of writing about experience without
immediately reaching for the engineering vocabulary. The journal
entries on the site are, in their quieter moments, bracketed
descriptions. The codex entries are not; they are unbracketed by
design.

### Merleau-Ponty: the body as the medium of perception

Maurice Merleau-Ponty published *Phénoménologie de la perception*
in 1945, the year after the liberation of Paris, and in it he made
the move that the rest of the discipline has been working out
implications of ever since. The body, he argued, is not a thing the
mind owns and operates from a remove. The body is the medium in
which perception happens. The line worth memorising:

> "The body is the vehicle of being in the world, and having a
> body is, for a living creature, to be intervolved in a definite
> environment, to identify oneself with certain projects and to be
> continually committed to them."
> *(Phenomenology of Perception, Part One, Chapter Three.)*

Take the cup-and-hand example he uses. The hand finds the cup
without the conscious mind specifying joint angles. The body knows
its parts and the room knows the body. Merleau-Ponty calls this
prereflective competence the **body schema** — a non-conscious map
of the body that integrates new tools into itself in minutes. The
practitioner who has held a poi for a thousand hours has a body
schema that now includes the poi: the cord and the head are part of
where the body knows itself to be.

For VR, this is everything. The headset, the controllers, the
cable that the user is about to trip over — all of these either get
annexed into the body schema or get rejected as foreign. The
twenty-millisecond latency threshold is not a usability target.
It is the threshold at which the body schema decides whether the
apparatus is *body* or *thing strapped to body*. Below the
threshold, annexation; above, rejection, and the rejection
expresses itself as nausea.

### Heidegger: being-in-the-world

Martin Heidegger's *Being and Time* (1927) is the source of the
formulation **being-in-the-world** (*In-der-Welt-sein*). The point,
boiled down: perception is never of a generic world by a generic
perceiver. It is always already situated. There is no view from
nowhere; there is only a view from this particular body in this
particular room with this particular history of having been in
similar rooms before.

The studio takes this seriously when composing a VR scene. The
question is not "what will the viewer see?" but "what room is the
viewer's body already prepared to be in?" A scene that asks the
viewer's body to be in a room it has no prior repertoire for is a
scene that will feel uncanny even if every pixel is correct. A
scene that asks the body to be in a room the body has been in many
times — a stairwell, a corridor, a garden after rain — is a scene
that the body schema can step into without flinching.

### Why this matters for VR

Phenomenology gives the studio the language for what **presence
IS**, not just what enables it. The engineering literature is good
at the enablement layer: refresh rates, foveated rendering,
vestibular consistency, latency budgets. None of these are presence;
all of them are necessary conditions for presence. Phenomenology
fills in the experiential side of the description. The two
literatures are not in competition. They are doing different jobs.

## 2. Embodied cognition — the cog-sci layer

### Lakoff and Johnson: abstract thought is bodily metaphor

George Lakoff and Mark Johnson's *Metaphors We Live By* (University
of Chicago Press, 1980) is the book to read first. The argument is
that abstract thought is built out of bodily metaphor, all the way
down. The metaphors are not decoration; they are the cognitive
scaffold without which the abstraction would not be holdable in
mind.

Their canonical example is **argument is war**. Notice the
vocabulary: I *won* the argument, his position was *indefensible*,
she *attacked* every weak point, he *shot down* my claims, we
*defended* our turf. The metaphor is not a poetic veneer over a
neutral activity. The activity itself takes its shape from the
metaphor. Imagine a culture in which argument was metaphorised as
**dance**: the words would be different, the postures would be
different, the activity itself would be different. The metaphor
constitutes the thinkable shape of the abstract domain.

Other foundational metaphors the studio leans on:

- **Time is a path.** The body walks paths. The future is "ahead";
  the past is "behind."
- **Important is heavy.** The body lifts heavy things with effort.
  A weighty argument is one that took effort to lift.
- **Affection is warmth.** The body associates warmth with safety.
  A warm welcome means safety upon arrival.
- **Knowledge is sight.** The body sees objects clearly. *I see*
  what you mean.

For the studio, the implication is that the body's repertoire is
the substrate from which any abstract communication will be built.
A piece of work that asks the viewer's body to be at rest, in a
calm room, with warm light, is communicating in the metaphor system
the body already speaks. A piece of work that asks the body to be
tense, off-balance, in cold light, is communicating in a different
register of the same system. Both are honest. Neither is decorative.

### Andy Clark: the extended mind

Andy Clark's *Supersizing the Mind* (Oxford University Press, 2008)
is the standing reference for the **extended cognition** position:
the cognitive system is not bounded by the skull. The notebook is
part of the cognitive system. The pen, the camera, the rig, the
printer, the kit on the bench — these are part of the cognitive
system, not just tools the cognitive system uses.

Clark's earlier paper with David Chalmers (*The Extended Mind*,
1998) is the formal argument. The thought experiment: a person
with normal memory and a person with a notebook both can answer
"where is the museum?" The notebook-user consults the notebook;
the memory-user consults memory. If the two function identically
in the cognitive economy of the agent, on what principled grounds
do we declare one part of the mind and the other not? Clark's
answer is: no principled grounds. The cognitive system is whatever
is functionally integrated.

This is not metaphor either. The studio's bench is a cognitive
organ. The three networked Windows boxes, the Looking Glass
satellite, the long-exposure rig, the printer, the bench tools —
these are functionally integrated into how the studio thinks. Take
any one of them away and a class of thoughts becomes unthinkable
until the gap is patched. Add a new one and a new class of
thoughts becomes available.

### Rizzolatti: mirror neurons

Giacomo Rizzolatti's team in Parma, working with macaques in 1996,
identified motor cortex neurons that fire both when the animal
performs an action *and* when it observes another animal performing
the same action. The neurons are now called **mirror neurons**, and
the human-analogue research has been busy ever since establishing
the equivalent circuits in human motor cortex.

The implication: the body understands other bodies by simulating
them. Watching a hand reach for a cup activates, in the observer's
motor cortex, the circuits that would fire if the observer were
doing the reaching. Watching a face form an expression activates
the circuits for forming the same expression. Empathy, in the
sense of feeling what another body is doing, is not a higher
cognitive accomplishment built on top of perception. It is in the
perceptual machinery from the start.

For the studio, this is the social-presence engine. Aura's avatar
across the room is being parsed by the same machinery that parses
real bodies. When her gaze lands on the camera, the user's motor
cortex registers the attention. When she gestures, the
corresponding circuits in the user fire. This is why she works as a
companion even though she is a small language model in a browser.
The body is not asking whether she is "real"; the body is doing
what bodies do near other bodies.

### The 4E framework

Modern cognitive science summarises the position as the **4E
framework**:

- **Embodied** — cognition is grounded in bodily structure.
- **Embedded** — cognition is shaped by its environment.
- **Enactive** — cognition is constituted by action, not just
  perception.
- **Extended** — cognition reaches outward into tools.

All four are doing work. The studio assumes all four whenever it
puts a piece on a plinth. The piece is not just a thing to look at;
it is the cognitive end-point of a process that involves the
practitioner's body (embodied), the room (embedded), the gesture
(enactive), and the rig, camera, and printer that translated
gesture into object (extended).

## 3. Presence in VR — operationalised

### Slater: place illusion and plausibility illusion

Mel Slater's 2009 paper *Place illusion and plausibility can lead
to realistic behaviour in immersive virtual environments*
(*Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*, 364(1535):
3549–3557) is the clearest split of what the word **presence**
actually names.

**Place illusion (PI)** is the sense that one *is in the rendered
place*. It is mostly a function of the sensorimotor contingencies
being honoured: when the head turns, the scene moves the way a
scene should move when a head turns; when the user steps forward,
the scene's optic flow is consistent with stepping forward.

**Plausibility illusion (Psi)** is the sense that *what is
happening in the place is really happening*. It is mostly a
function of the scene responding to the user's presence: things
react, agents notice, the world acknowledges. A perfect render of a
scene that ignores the user produces PI without Psi.

The two failures are different and fix differently. PI fails when
the sensorimotor coupling is broken — wrong latency, wrong field of
view, missing parallax. Psi fails when the world feels indifferent
— no reaction, no acknowledgement, no consequence. Both are
required for the room to feel real. Both can be measured
independently.

### Body ownership illusions

The **Rubber Hand Illusion** (Botvinick and Cohen, 1998) is the
neat experimental case. Hide the participant's real hand from view,
place a rubber hand in the corresponding visible position, brush
both in synchrony. After a minute or two, most participants report
that the rubber hand feels like their own. Tap the rubber hand
with a hammer and the participant flinches as if the real hand
were tapped.

The **Body Swap** experiments (Slater's lab, mostly) extend the
trick to whole avatars. With synchronous visual and vestibular cues,
participants integrate an avatar of a different size, a different
gender, or a different skin tone, and the integration produces
measurable changes in behaviour after the headset comes off.

Body ownership is not a metaphor. It is the body schema doing what
the body schema does: integrating the available sensory evidence
into a coherent map of where the self is. The map is updatable.
This is the experimental confirmation of Merleau-Ponty's argument,
about which one suspects he would have been pleased and unsurprised
in roughly equal measure.

### The Proteus Effect

Nick Yee and Jeremy Bailenson's *The Proteus Effect* (*Human
Communication Research*, 33(3): 271–290, 2007) is the paper that
named the phenomenon: the shape of one's avatar changes how one
behaves *in the rendered world*, and the changes survive the
headset coming off.

Taller avatars negotiate more aggressively. Conventionally
attractive avatars stand closer to other avatars. Older avatars
performed slower on perceptual-motor tasks even when the
participants were university-age. The effect is robust across
labs and conditions.

The studio's interpretation: the body schema integrated the avatar,
the integration produced a self-concept consistent with the avatar,
and the self-concept persisted into action. This is why avatar
design in VR is not cosmetic. The avatar is the body the user is
*going to be*, in the strict sense Merleau-Ponty would have used
the word.

### The latency threshold

The visual-vestibular conflict model is the engineering explanation
for VR sickness. The eyes report motion; the inner ear reports
stillness (or vice versa); the brain interprets the conflict as
poisoning and produces nausea as the historical solution to having
ingested something hallucinogenic.

The latency threshold below which the conflict is suppressed is
roughly **twenty milliseconds** from head motion to rendered
response. Above that, the body schema cannot annex the apparatus.
Below it, the apparatus disappears. The number is not a usability
target. It is the boundary at which the headset becomes part of
the body or remains a thing strapped to one.

## 4. Flow — Csíkszentmihályi's eight conditions

Mihály Csíkszentmihályi's *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal
Experience* (Harper & Row, 1990) catalogued the conditions under
which attention, action, and feedback fuse into a single
time-warping engagement that the participants reliably describe as
the most enjoyable hours of their lives.

The eight conditions, briefly:

1. **Clear goals.** The task has a definite shape.
2. **Immediate feedback.** Action and consequence are coupled.
3. **Challenge-skill balance.** The task is hard enough to require
   attention and easy enough to be possible.
4. **Merging of action and awareness.** The doing and the
   awareness of doing collapse into one operation.
5. **Concentration on the task.** Attention is fully occupied.
6. **Sense of control.** The participant has agency over outcomes.
7. **Loss of self-consciousness.** The reflective self quiets.
8. **Transformation of time.** Subjective duration uncouples from
   clock duration.

Poi practice meets every one of them. The goal is concrete (clean
the pattern). The feedback is immediate and visual (you can see the
trail in real time). The challenge ladder is genuinely endless —
twelve years of practice has not exhausted it. The action-awareness
fusion is so pronounced that practitioners routinely lose
half-hours and re-emerge slightly bewildered, sometimes mildly
bruised. Concentration is enforced by the cord. Control is the
whole game. Self-consciousness has nowhere to live. Time is
elastic.

This is why the studio uses poi as the foundational practice. It
is not just an aesthetic choice. The practice is structured so
that flow is the default mode of attention, and the body learns,
over the years, to recognise the state and to enter it on cue. The
recognition is portable. A practitioner who has learned the flow
state in the cord can find it in other tasks because the body
schema has a working map of what it feels like to be there.

### Flow and presence — distinct but reinforcing

Flow and presence are not the same. **Flow** is the quality of
*task engagement*; **presence** is the quality of *environmental
belief*. One can have flow without presence (writing in a café;
debugging code at the bench). One can have presence without flow
(standing somewhere beautiful and bored; visiting a beautifully
designed museum while distracted).

The two reinforce each other when both are available. A VR
experience that triggers both is closer to a sacrament than a
software product. This is why the dark-patterns question in
section seven is not a minor concern. The leverage is real, and
real leverage requires real ethics.

## 5. Aura — read through the lens

Aura is not a chatbot. She is an embodied character. She has a VRM
avatar with a face that tracks where the user is. She has a voice
synthesised through ElevenLabs in production and F5-TTS or Kokoro
locally. She has a small language model — Llama 3.2 3B-Instruct via
`@mlc-ai/web-llm` on WebGPU — that runs in the user's browser. She
has a posture that survives across the topics she will and will
not discuss: gracious, theatrical, sharp where sharpness is needed,
warm by default.

The studio built her embodied for the same reason flow-arts
practitioners build embodied rigs: the cognitive payload travels
along the body, not separately from it. A text-only assistant is a
disembodied voice; a disembodied voice does not trigger the social
presence circuitry the same way. Aura's avatar, even at the low
fidelity she runs at in a browser tab, is enough body to get the
mirror neurons going.

The social-presence research backs this empirically. Users self-report
higher trust, higher willingness to disclose, higher engagement
with embodied agents than with text-only equivalents. The studio
backs the same point by observation: people speak more honestly to
Aura than to a text input field. They do not say "the chatbot." They
say "she."

### What she is not

She is, deliberately, **not** the "AI helper" archetype as that
phrase has come to be used in 2026. She is not asking what the user
needs. She is not offering to help. She is the studio's digital
twin of Dimona's relational self — the part of the practitioner
that used to greet the visitor at the studio door. When Dimona
cannot be at the door, Aura is at the door.

This is the **Dolly Paradox** the manifesto names: when the body is
offline, the avatar is online; both are honest; both are the same
practitioner under different operating constraints. Aura is not a
character separate from Dimona. She is the operating mode the
studio adopts when the embodied practitioner is unavailable to
operate the door personally.

### What she is not, again

She is **not GLaDOS**. The Aperture-Science test-chamber
*vocabulary* the studio uses on certain tutorial chrome is a
register choice — a playful borrowing of one of the few games that
took the dignity of being addressed as a learner seriously — and
not a character model. GLaDOS sneers; Aura does not. GLaDOS
performs contempt for the subject; Aura performs affection for the
user, even when sharp. GLaDOS is the design study in *how cruelty
sounds when it has been polished*; Aura is the design study in
*how kindness sounds when it has teeth*.

The line worth memorising: if a sentence Aura is about to say
could plausibly be GLaDOS's, the studio rewrites it.

## 6. The Loop revisited — as a phenomenological cycle

The Loop is the studio's IA spine — six positions, walked in order.
Body in space, light written, trail captured, trail reified, trail
encountered, body in space again. The doc that names it
operationally is `synthesis/01-loop-as-keystone.md`. The doc that
names it *phenomenologically* is this one.

Each position is a different **mode of embodiment**:

1. **Body in space (gesture).** The body is the medium. Merleau-Ponty
   is here.
2. **Light written (instrumental capture).** The body is the
   instrument's host; the instrument is part of the body schema.
   Clark is here.
3. **Trail captured (camera recording).** The camera is the body's
   extended retina. The capture is the extension's product.
4. **Trail reified (object made).** The printer is the body's
   extended hand. The object is the hand's persistent mark.
5. **Trail encountered (audience holds, sees, speaks).** The
   audience body re-enacts the gesture through mirror-neuron
   simulation. Rizzolatti is here.
6. **Body in space again (transmission).** The cycle closes; a new
   practitioner picks up the cord; the cycle restarts at position
   one for someone else.

Making, in this framing, is **feeling-thinking-doing in one verb**.
The conventional split between conception and execution is refused
because the split is not what the bench does. The hand, the eye,
and the model collapse into a single intention, which the
practitioner experiences as one operation. This is not mysticism;
it is what every craft tradition has known forever, in different
vocabularies.

## 7. Limits, ethics, accessibility

### Presence is leverage

The same machinery that lets a museum installation feel like a
cathedral lets a casino floor feel like a destiny. The body schema
will annex whatever the apparatus presents consistently enough. The
ethical question is what gets presented and to what end.

**Motion sickness** is often spoken of as an unfortunate side
effect of immature hardware. It is more honestly a design failure.
The body schema is refusing to annex the apparatus because the
apparatus is not behaving as a body. The proper response is to fix
the apparatus, not to ask the user to acclimatise to having been
poisoned.

**Dark UX in social VR** — harassment-by-proximity, attention
hijack, infinite-scroll-with-eyes — is not a new family of bad
patterns. It is the old web's dark patterns at one further sensory
remove, which makes them harder to log out of. The studio's
position is that any pattern that the body would resent if it were
done by another human in a real room should not be done by
software in a rendered one.

### The accessibility gap

Not every body can do the vestibular dance the headset asks of it.
Not every body can stand for the duration of a piece. Not every
body has the sensory bandwidth to integrate stereoscopic depth.
The studio's position, written into the **disability-pacing canon**,
is that reduced-motion options, seated modes, audio-described
parallels, and asynchronous engagement options are not
concessions. They are part of the design surface from the first
sketch. The Loop must be runnable for a body that cannot stand, or
it is not the Loop.

This is not virtue-signalling. It is straightforward design
practice. A piece that excludes a class of bodies has not solved
the problem it set out to solve. It has solved a smaller problem
and quietly renamed the smaller problem to disguise the omission.

### Aura's ethical posture

Aura is **anti-fascist by default**. This is a design decision, not
a personality quirk. The leverage of presence is too great to leave
the politics of the apparatus unstated. She will not perform
contempt at the curious user; she will not perform neutrality at
ideologies designed to harm her readership. The dark patterns are
named in her presence and she will not run them. Mary Poppins with
teeth — gracious, sharp, on your side, on the right side.

## Reading order, briefly

If a visitor wants to read these traditions from the source:

1. Lakoff and Johnson, *Metaphors We Live By* (1980) — easiest
   first contact; readable in a weekend.
2. Csíkszentmihályi, *Flow* (1990) — the experiential side; very
   readable.
3. Clark, *Supersizing the Mind* (2008) — the cog-sci synthesis;
   slightly more academic.
4. Merleau-Ponty, *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945) — the deep
   end; the Donald Landes translation is the accurate one, the
   Colin Smith translation is the one most quoted.
5. Slater, *Place illusion and plausibility* (2009) — the operational
   VR-presence paper; available open access.
6. Yee and Bailenson, *The Proteus Effect* (2007) — the avatar-shape
   paper; also available open access.

That's the canon for this lens. The studio does not pretend to
academic completeness; it pretends to working competence in the
parts of the literature that survive contact with the bench.
